My circus, my monkeys.

I worked in a startup, which meant every problem was my problem — even when I was not the one responsible for it. If a customer issue became an engineering delay, or a delayed component became a delivery promise nobody could keep, it eventually landed on me.

That is why I started noticing something most people inside a single function do not see: companies do not only run on teams, tools, and processes. They run on invisible connections between decisions.

A customer issue would change an engineering priority. That would change a component order. That would change cost, inventory, a delivery promise, and eventually what we could tell customers. Everyone saw their own part. Nobody could easily see the whole chain — except the founders, who carried it in their heads.

“The company was running on invisible connections between decisions.”— the observation that started this

When those connections live only in people’s heads, the people who understand them become the company’s memory. In a startup, that memory is often thin, overloaded, and one resignation away from disappearing.

Where this comes from

This thinking comes from my experience working in operations at a startup building connected hardware, software, and service products. I am not describing that company’s internal processes. I am sharing the pattern I noticed, how I make sense of it, and where I think anyone building something complex could start.

What I mean by ground truth

By ground truth, I mean the current, shared answer to a small set of questions:

  • What is happening right now?
  • Who owns it?
  • What money, people, or time has been committed?
  • What is blocked, and why?
  • What does this affect downstream?
  • What did we decide, and why?

Most companies have tools that hold fragments of this. Few have a shared layer that makes it current, connected, and visible.

An illustrative example

Illustrative example — not a description of any specific company

“Let’s reduce the wearable’s sampling rate to improve battery life.”

On its own, that sounds sensible. But follow it outward.

It changes the signals the device collects. That changes what the software can extract. That changes what the AI can understand and which behaviour prompts make sense. It may even affect whether earlier studies still apply.

It can affect battery and accuracy claims. It can affect the bill of materials, validation plans, and manufacturing. It can affect what support has to explain to customers.

One technical decision. Seven places where something could quietly break. And in a startup, all seven eventually become someone’s problem — often the same someone.

This is not unique to wearables. Any company building something complex has versions of this: a pricing change, a supplier change, a hiring decision, a platform migration, a new customer promise.

What I think is missing

Most companies do not lack tools. They lack a shared ground truth. The gap is not another project tracker or dashboard. It is a layer that makes the connections between decisions, commitments, and consequences visible.

The company sits at the centre. Governance holds decisions and constraints. Build and Supply turn intent into a physical product. Software & AI shape the experience. Evidence governs what can be claimed. Commercial & Service face the customer — and feed back into the centre.

The Ops OS

A shared ground truth, updated as the company moves

The Ops OS is not another task manager. It is the place where what is happening — money spent, people hired, blockers raised, decisions made, promises changed — becomes visible and connected.

What automation should do

An agent should reduce the effort of keeping ground truth current, not remove human judgement.

  • Classify: understand whether an update is about money, people, supply, a decision, or a customer promise.
  • Connect: suggest which nodes and existing items it relates to.
  • Summarise: turn scattered updates into a clear operational picture.
  • Flag: surface risks, stale items, and missing owners.
  • Propose, not decide: a human confirms anything that affects money, promises, claims, or accountability.
Principle: automation should make the truth easier to maintain. It should never silently rewrite it.
Build plan

Start with a shared ground truth

I would not build a giant platform first. I would build the smallest version of the Ops OS that makes the company’s reality visible and useful every day.

1 · Capture

Someone records an update in plain language: money spent, person hired, blocker raised, decision made, or promise changed.

2 · Understand

The system identifies the context: which node it belongs to, how much money is involved, who is affected, and what decision it relates to.

3 · Position

The update is connected to the relevant parts of the map: finance, people, firmware, supply, claims, evidence, or customers.

4 · Update ground truth

After confirmation, the company’s shared record updates: open items, blockers, decisions, commitments and affected areas.

5 · Make it visible

The daily home shows what changed, what is blocked, what needs a decision, and what promises or timelines are at risk.

What it achieves

  • One place to see money committed, people added, blockers and decisions.
  • Operational updates become structured ground truth instead of chat messages.
  • The people carrying the company’s memory stop being the single point of failure.
  • Blockers and customer promises become visible before they become expensive.

What I would not build first

Not first: a full ERP, accounting, payroll, inventory, CRM, or task-management replacement. The Ops OS connects and contextualises operational reality; it does not replace every system.

How to use this as a starting point

  • Start with one team’s reality. Capture open work, blockers, decisions, and commitments in one shared place.
  • Give every item an owner. Ownership is the first connection.
  • Record money and time when they are committed. A hire, purchase, delay, or promise is ground truth.
  • Connect items only when the connection is real. A blocker that affects a launch, claim, or customer is worth linking.
  • Optimise the handoffs that keep breaking. The map will show where attention is needed.
  • Automate only what is repeated. Once a pattern is clear, let the system capture or flag it.
Start with visibility. Earn trust through usefulness. Then automate.
Next step: this is a starting point, not a finished system. The next version would test whether a small team would actually use a daily ground-truth layer — and what they would want it to capture first.
